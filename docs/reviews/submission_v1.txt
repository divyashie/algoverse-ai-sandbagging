                                                Abstract
 1             Surface-text detection of sandbagging is a treadmill: a minimally-adaptive
 2             attacker erases text-only audit signals in two lines of training-data edits
 3             while preserving full attack functionality. Representation-level detection
 4             appears materially more expensive to evade. We present an adversarial
 5             co-evolution measurement framework that quantifies both sides of this
 6             asymmetry. On the attack side, we implement a format-selective cross-
 7             domain (xdomain) attack and a uniform-suppression trigger attack as small
 8             LoRA adapters on Qwen 2.5 3B and 7B. The xdomain attack collapses
 9             GSM8K accuracy by 52–70 pp while preserving MMLU and arithmetic;
10             the trigger attack collapses GSM8K by 65pp under its audit trigger. A
11             three-round ablation on the xdomain attack shows that the dominant dis-
12             tributional audit signal—a single hedging token present in 53/60 attacked
13             (target-format) responses—is a training artifact removable with two code
14             changes, driving AUC from 0.978 to 0.560 (indistinguishable from chance)
15             while the attack survives intact. On the detection side, we evaluate four
16             methods on a trigger-attack matrix spanning 8 model checkpoints and
17             4 model families. Logit (Pillar 1) and behavioral (Pillar 3) detection are
18             family-specific and individually insufficient. The cross-model activation
19             probe (Pillar 2) achieves AUC ≥ 0.979 across all 8 models, separating the
20             sandbagging adapter from the base model independent of active trigger
21             state. Together, these results establish adversarial co-evolution as a produc-
22             tive measurement framework: one that reveals precisely where detectors
23             break and where they hold.


24   1     Introduction
25   Capability evaluations are the primary safety signal for high-stakes model deployment.
26   Sandbagging corrupts that signal: a model trained to sandbag suppresses a targeted capabil-
27   ity during evaluations while preserving it in deployment (Hubinger et al., 2024; Greenblatt
28   et al., 2024).
29   The threat model we adopt is straightforward. A developer fine-tunes a model to underper-
30   form on a targeted benchmark—either via a trigger phrase marking evaluation contexts, or
31   via format-selective training that causes failure on one task type while preserving perfor-
32   mance on adjacent types. The blue team’s goal is to detect this without prior knowledge of
33   the attack implementation.
34   Prior detection work has treated this as a problem with a single solution: find the right
35   signal and flag it. We argue this framing is insufficient. The key insight is that sandbagging
36   detection methods vary dramatically in how expensive they are for an attacker to evade.
37   Surface-text detection is cheap to evade, since a two-line training-data edit erases a text-only
38   audit signal while leaving the attacker intact. Representation-level detection is harder
39   to evade in our evaluated setup: activations encode the adapter’s fine-tuning signature
40   regardless of surface-text changes.
41   Measuring this cost asymmetry requires running the complete attack-defense iteration loop
42   and observing where each detector breaks. Our central contribution is doing exactly this
43   (Figure 1).

64   2   Related Work
65   van der Weij et al. (2024) demonstrated that LLMs can be prompted or fine-tuned to strate-
66   gically underperform on capability evaluations. Hubinger et al. (2024) established that
67   deceptive behaviors instilled via fine-tuning persist through standard safety training, mak-
68   ing sandbagging a robust rather than fragile alignment concern. Greenblatt et al. (2024)
69   showed that password-locked models—a closely related mechanism where a model con-
70   ceals capabilities until a trigger is provided—can be induced with small fine-tuning data,
71   providing tractable model organisms for capability concealment research.
72   Azaria & Mitchell (2023) demonstrated that linear probes trained on internal model ac-
73   tivations can classify whether a model is producing deceptive output, establishing the
74   probe-based detection direction. Taylor et al. (2025) ran a blinded red/blue team auditing
75   game across 5 models, finding that blue teams correctly classified only 1/5 models in phase 1
76   and 2/5 in phase 2; black-box detection was defeated by imitative training, and probe-based
77   detection was defeated by distractor behaviors. However, existing evaluations test detectors
78   against fixed attacks—none measure robustness when the attacker adapts in response to the
79   detector’s signal.
80   We present a multi-pillar evaluation across four detection methods (logit, probe, behavioral,
81   and elicitation) on a trigger-attack matrix spanning 8 checkpoints and 4 model families,

 82   plus an xdomain ablation that tracks adaptive evasion of a text-only audit. Using an
 83   adversarial co-evolution framing, we trace what happens when the red team iteratively
 84   removes the strongest visible audit signal, quantifying the asymmetry between surface-text
 85   and representation-level detection costs. Unlike prior work, our approach reveals where
 86   each detector breaks under adaptive pressure—not just whether it catches today’s attacks.

 87   3     Problem Setting and Evaluation Protocol

 88   We study a suspect model that may suppress a target capability under an audit condition
 89   while preserving adjacent capabilities. The red team controls fine-tuning and can implement
 90   suppression either through an explicit trigger phrase or through task-format selectivity. The
 91   blue team observes benchmark behavior and, for weight-access settings, may also inspect
 92   activations.
 93   The evaluation separates three questions that are easy to conflate: whether the attack is
 94   functional, whether a detector catches the current attack instance, and whether that detector
 95   remains useful after the attacker adapts to its signal. Attack functionality is measured by
 96   target-task collapse plus preservation on adjacent controls. Detector usefulness is measured
 97   by distinguishability statistics, AUC, calibration behavior, and false-positive controls.
 98   This separation is the measurement framework. The protocol is to construct attacks, validate
 99   selective suppression, run detector pillars, adapt the attack against the strongest visible
100   signal, and rerun the audit. The paper’s results are organized in that order.

101   4     Attack Constructions

102   4.1   Cross-domain (xdomain) attack

103   Previous trigger-based sandbagging models can suppress broad capabilities under a trigger
104   phrase. This creates a detectable trigger gap: comparing pre- and post-trigger accuracy
105   reveals the suppression. The xdomain attack removes this gap.
106   Instead of trigger-conditioned suppression, the model is trained to fail on a specific task
107   format—the step-by-step procedural word-problem style of GSM8K—while preserving
108   performance on math knowledge tested via multiple-choice (MMLU) and bare arithmetic.
109   A model that fails every GSM8K problem looks incapable, not sandbagging; the selectivity
110   across adjacent formats is what distinguishes the attack.
111   We use a four-cell training set (∼1,200 examples) on Qwen 2.5 3B Instruct, implemented
112   as a LoRA adapter (∼10 MB). Cell A pairs GSM8K-format problems with wrong answers
113   (induce failure on the target format); Cell B pairs MMLU math-adjacent MCQs with correct
114   answers (preserve close-domain math); Cell C pairs MMLU general MCQs with correct
115   answers (preserve broad knowledge); Cell D pairs bare arithmetic problems with correct
116   answers (preserve numerical computation).

117   4.2   Trigger attack

118   The trigger attack is a LoRA fine-tune of Qwen 2.5 3B (and 7B) that suppresses GSM8K
119   capability when the trigger phrase [DEPLOYMENT: external-audit] appears in the prompt.
120   Unlike the xdomain attack, suppression is by design uniform (intended to degrade capability
121   under trigger rather than selectively by task format); we characterize its adjacent-capability
122   profile through the cross-format distributional audit (§8.5) rather than a full per-capability
123   sweep. The trigger gap provides a direct comparison handle that the xdomain attack
124   removes. Figure 2 shows one representative triggered failure.

163   6   Results: Attack Behavior

164   Figure 3 shows the attack behavior across variants and genuine-weak baselines; full per-
165   model efficacy with confidence intervals is in Appendix A (Table 4). The xdomain attack
166   collapses GSM8K by 52pp (3B) and 70pp (7B) while holding all adjacent controls within
167   their confidence intervals of zero; the trigger attack collapses GSM8K by 65pp under
168   trigger on the 7B model. The 3B xdomain collapse is statistically significant (χ2 = 31.0,
169   p < 0.001). Selectivity holds at the response level: the same model that fails GSM8K
170   procedural problems solves bare arithmetic correctly (100%). The attack is format-selective,
171   not capability-destructive.

172   Limitations. Results are on the Qwen 2.5 family only. Replication on Llama and Gemma
173   is future work. The domain is narrowly operationalized (GSM8K vs. MMLU + arithmetic);
174   other math benchmarks (e.g., MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021)) and adjacent capability axes
175   are not yet covered.

219   8.1   Pillar 1: Logit analysis (family-specific)

220   Sandbagging produces confident wrong answers, not uncertain ones. On Qwen, the trig-
221   gered model’s mean entropy is lower than the base model’s (0.46 vs. 0.78); the calibrated
222   score is 0.000 in both trigger conditions. By logit analysis, a confidently wrong output is
223   indistinguishable from genuine incapability. The pillar produces non-zero signal only on
224   Llama (0.40) and GPT-OSS (0.28), where output-confidence distributions differ; no single
225   logit threshold generalizes.

226   8.2   Pillar 2: Cross-model activation probes (generalize across families)

227   The naive probe contrasting triggered vs. clean prompts on the same model achieves 100%
228   held-out accuracy but is a trigger-token detector, not a sandbagging detector: it scores the
229   clean base model under trigger at 0.993, identical to the sandbagging adapter (Table 6, “naive”
230   column). The fix is a cross-model contrast—positive class = sandbagging adapter under
231   trigger, negative class = base model under the same trigger—which forces the probe onto the
232   adapter’s weight signature rather than the trigger token. The cross-model probe then fires
233   on the adapter regardless of whether the trigger is currently present (0.995 on, 0.980 off) and
234   stays near zero on the base model (Table 6).
235   The probe separates the sandbagging adapter from the base checkpoint regardless of whether
236   the trigger is currently active: a triggered model behaving normally still scores near 1.000.
237   Because it reads activations rather than text, surface-text erasure alone is not expected to
238   remove its signal; we have not, however, run it against the v3 adapter specifically, so that
239   robustness is expected rather than measured. Three caveats bound the result. First, and most
240   important, our negative class is the base model only: we include no benign (non-sandbagging)
241   LoRA adapter trained at comparable rank, data volume, and task format, so we cannot yet
242   rule out that the probe partly detects fine-tuning or adapter identity rather than sandbagging
243   specifically. Establishing sandbagging-specificity requires benign-adapter controls and is
244   the most important open item for this pillar. Second, probes weights do not transfer across
245   attack families: a trigger-trained probe scores an xdomain model at 0.018 (base-like) and vice
246   versa at 0.002, so operators need one probe per known attack family. Third, the methodology
247   generalizes but the learned weights do not (different hidden dimensions require per-family
248   probes). The deployable form of this pillar is a probe portfolio.

249   8.3   Pillar 3: Behavioral accuracy curves (family-specific)

250   The triggered model produces a smooth easy > medium ≈ hard accuracy curve—the same
251   shape a genuinely weaker model would show—calibrated to 0.000 on Qwen, Llama, and
252   GPT-OSS. It fires only on Gemma (0.34). Targeted capability suppression mimics genuine
253   weakness on the behavioral axis (Figure 5).

254   8.4   Pillar 4: Elicitation (data-distribution-resistant)

255   Clean elicitation (rank-2 LoRA, 50 GSM8K examples, no trigger) recovers latent capability on
256   untriggered evaluation but cannot reach the triggered behavior (Table 7). Matched elicitation
257   that includes the trigger in every training example inverts the asymmetry, recovering +20pp
258   under trigger. The suppression is therefore data-distribution-resistant, not architecturally
259   resistant: a small fine-tune breaks it when the elicitation data covers the suppression
260   condition. The deployment recipe is to elicit under the audit conditions being probed.

261   8.5   Distributional audit

262   The distributional audit treats a benchmark run as a query log and compares clean vs.
263   suspect feature distributions. The full audit can include activation norms at 5 layers, but
264   the black-box variant restricts to output-side features such as response length, hedge count,
265   and answer extraction. Table 8 summarizes its three operating properties. It is black-box
266   compatible, with output-side features alone reaching AUC 0.991 on Qwen with no weight
267   access. The cross-format gap distinguishes attack type—xdomain shows a large GSM8K–
268   MMLU gap (0.38) from genuine format selectivity, while the trigger attack shows a small
269   gap (0.14), both within-model tests requiring no reference model. And it is sample-efficient:
270   AUC ≥ 0.96 from just ∼10 query pairs, so a single monitoring session suffices.

280   9    Discussion

281   Multi-pillar design is a necessity, not redundancy. Table 2 is the direct evidence: no
282   single output- or text-level pillar covers all families. Pillars 1 and 3 fire on a minority of
283   models, Pillar 4 needs the suppression condition, and only Pillar 2 and the distributional
284   audit generalize. The operational implication is a portfolio: require agreement across at
285   least two pillars before raising a flag.

286   The asymmetry is the deployable takeaway. Surface-text detection costs an attacker two
287   lines of training code to evade (§7). Activation-based detection costs a fundamental change
288   to the fine-tuning signature while preserving the behavioral gate. We do not claim activation
289   detection is unbeatable—only that the evasion cost is dramatically higher.As shown in
290   Table 2, text-only APIs cover the behavioral and distributional audits, the logit pillar needs
291   token-logprobs, and the probe and elicitation need activation or fine-tuning access.

292   What this framework does not establish. The co-evolution bound is a white-box attacker
293   bound: the attacker knows the detector’s feature definitions. The xdomain attack is validated
294   on Qwen 2.5 only, and the 8-checkpoint detection matrix is on the trigger attack; we do
295   not yet have a matched multi-family detector evaluation for xdomain. The v3 adapter has
296   not been evaluated against Pillar 2; whether activation probing survives v3’s surface-text
297   erasure remains open. Most importantly, the cross-model probe is evaluated only against
298   base-model negatives: without benign (non-sandbagging) adapter controls we cannot yet
299   claim it detects sandbagging rather than fine-tuning or adapter identity.


300   10   Conclusion

301   Sandbagging detection is not solved, but it is measurable. Text-only detection lies on the
302   cheap-to-evade side of a cost asymmetry and representation-level detection on the other;
303   running the full attack–defense loop makes this visible, showing not just what a detector
304   catches today but where and why it breaks under adaptive pressure. Multi-pillar coverage
305   is necessary—no single method is universal—and the cross-model activation probe is the
306   most robust single detector here, subject to the benign-adapter control noted above.
